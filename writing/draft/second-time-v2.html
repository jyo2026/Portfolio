What Working on a Seed Deal with a Second-Time Founder Taught Me About Client Management

She had done this before. She knew the process. That's what made it interesting.

I recently worked on a seed round for a founder who was raising from a serious table — Antler, Piper, and individual angels including Kunal Shah and Gaurav Munjal. She had spent time at a funded startup and understood how VC deals worked. She wasn't signing an SHA for the first time as she had been part of funding rounds before.

That, I quickly realised, creates its own challenge during client management.

Half-knowledge is a different challenge than no knowledge

First-time founders defer to their lawyers because they know they don't know. Experienced founders arrive with a working mental model of the process. They understand what the documents are. They have a feel for the negotiation flow.

What they often lack is the legal lens to analyse specific issues. Why does this thing need to slow down before it moves? That gap — between knowing the process and understanding why — is where the real friction lives.

And it's silent. It doesn't come with a disclaimer.

The holding email that became a flashpoint

We had received redlines from investor counsel. Most changes were routine. But my senior needed to review one specific legal point before we reverted. As is a standard practice on such deals, I sent a holding email.

The founder felt we were stalling. She escalated this to my senior.

My senior did not apologise for the email. Instead, she explained the legal point to the founder. That moment stayed with me because there's a version of that conversation where the lawyer caves and sends the document out without the check, keeps the client comfortable. That's not client service. That's a lawyer outsourcing their judgment because of pressure.

The holding email was the job.

Founders optimise for momentum. Lawyers optimise for risk minimisation. Navigating this inherent dichotomy is not an operational failure, but rather the core function of legal counsel. A lawyer who can't hold a position their client disagrees with isn't adding value. They're just drafting.

But she also moved the deal in ways we couldn't

When investor counsel stalled on points that didn't need to be fought over, she picked up the phone and called the investors directly. The deal moved. There was commercial instinct at work — a founder who knew when to push and when to let go. No amount of lawyer correspondence could have replicated that.

On my senior's birthday, we had gone out Friday evening. I messaged asking if we could pause follow-ups for the night; we had a Saturday call locked in. She agreed without hesitation. That grace, in a fast-moving deal, matters.

What this deal clarified

A lawyer's job in early-stage deals isn't just to draft and negotiate. Sometimes it's to hold a position your client disagrees with, explain it clearly, and move forward together once they get it. How you hold that position matters as much as the fact that you hold it. Condescension closes the conversation. Clarity keeps it open.

Second-time founders aren't easier clients. They are more interesting ones. They'll test your judgment in ways first-timers won't. That is exactly what teaches you to be a better transactional lawyer.

Personal observations from practice. Nothing here constitutes legal advice. Names and deal details have been kept deliberately general.
