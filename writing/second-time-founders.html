What a Second-Time Founder Taught Me About Client Management

He had done this before. And that, I quickly realised, was exactly what made it interesting.

I recently worked on a seed round for a founder who was raising from a serious table — Antler, Piper and other leading Tier 1 investors. He had been part of funding rounds before and understood how VC deals worked. He wasn't signing an SHA for the first time.

I assumed that would make things easier. It didn't, not in the way I expected.

Half-knowledge is its own kind of challenge

First-time founders mostly defer to their lawyers, because they know they don't know the process. Founders who have been through a round before arrive with a working mental model of the process. He understood the flow, the documents, the sequence of things. What he did not always have was a feel for why certain things needed to slow down before they moved.

That gap is quieter than the first-timer gap. It doesn't announce itself.

The holding email that became a flashpoint

We'd gotten redlines back from investor counsel. Most of it was routine, but one point needed my senior to look at it properly before we responded. So I sent a holding email, saying we'd revert once our team had reviewed it. Standard stuff on any deal.

The founder read it as stalling and went straight to my senior.

What stuck with me is what my senior didn't do. She didn't apologise for the delay or rush the review to smooth things over. She got on a call and walked the founder through exactly why that point needed a second look. There's a version of that conversation where the lawyer just caves, sends the document out to keep the client happy in the moment. That's the easy way to lose the ability to say no later.

A lawyer who folds the moment a client pushes back isn't managing the relationship. They're just postponing the next fight to a worse moment.

But he also moved the deal in ways we couldn't

To be fair to him, he moved things in ways we couldn't have. When investor counsel sat on a point that didn't need fighting over, he picked up the phone and called the investor directly. Deal moved by evening. That instinct for when to push and when to just let something go isn't something a lawyer can replicate from behind an email thread.

He was a tough but reasonable client. I once messaged him on a Friday night asking if we could hold follow-ups till the weekend since it was my senior's birthday. He said yes without making it a thing.

What this deal clarified

A lawyer's job in these rounds isn't only drafting and negotiating. Sometimes it's holding a position your client is unhappy with, explaining it properly, and letting them come around instead of folding because it's easier in the moment.

Second-time founders will test that more than first-timers will, not because they're harder to work with, but because they know enough to push back.

Personal observations from practice. Nothing here constitutes legal advice. Names and deal details have been kept deliberately general.
